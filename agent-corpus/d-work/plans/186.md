## The audit's own structural finding, kept
The scan erases string constants, so it is a *hand-rolled-parametrization* detector, not a duplicate
detector: by construction it cannot emit a true duplicate, because a true duplicate has the same
strings too. That is why all 16 of its groups adjudicate to parametrization and why the one true
duplicate in the repo was found only by a second, strings-intact pass. Recorded so the next audit
starts from the right detector. Its stated `stmts >= 2` filter also hid 120 one-statement bodies
holding the two largest collapses.

## Rule-10
- **Path:** two PRs. Fix the three assertions that cannot fail, delete the one true duplicate while
  keeping its claim in a comment, collapse the two clusters that earn it — one the largest in the
  repo, one adding the three missing-header cases it exposes — and leave 20 cosmetic collapses and
  the `test_live_*` question as backlog rows.
- **Counter:** take the whole audit. 22 tables, 39 method bodies, one sweep while the findings are
  fresh and the file map is in hand; splitting it means re-reading 21 files later for a change already
  adjudicated, and a backlog row is where findings go to die.
- **Reconciliation:** the counter is right that the cost of re-reading is real and that backlog rows
  rot. It is wrong about what the audit established. It established a *detector* — string-erased body
  identity — whose every hit is a parametrization candidate, which is a preference about how tests are
  written, not a defect in what they check. Three assertions that cannot fail are defects and belong
  in a PR today. One duplicate is a deletion. Two collapses pay for themselves, one of them in
  coverage. The other 20 change no verdict anywhere, and a 39-method rewrite across five crafts is
  precisely the sweep whose review cost buys nothing measurable — the sub-agent says so itself.
