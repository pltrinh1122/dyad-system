# Plan #178 — falsify: the chat surface falls under `ui-design` or `ux-design`

Base commit of `main`: 6245bea. Refs #174 (the settlement that defined `ui-design`), #177 (its
backlog row), #113 (`concise-mode`, the most recent deliberate change to this very surface).

## Intent as read
The claim names the conversation between Operator and Agent — counter-prompts, dispositions, the
shape of a reply — and asks whether it belongs to `ui-design`, or to a `ux-design` craft by
analogy. Both branches are attacked against the corpus as it stands, not against the words.

## Falsification
**Claim:** the current chat surface between agent and Operator falls under `ui-design` or `ux-design`.

| # | Attack | Result | Survivor |
|---|--------|--------|----------|
| 1 | Test the word `surface`: is the chat one? | **Refuted** | `surface` is a defined craft term (`crafts/sysarch/vocabulary/CRAFT.md`): "a viewing form the Operator reads: ERD, system schema, entity cards, events; each has one projector, in one craft", and a `projection` is "a mechanical rendering of parsed corpus data onto a surface, generated by a projector, never hand-drawn, never committed". The chat has no projector, renders no parsed corpus data, and is not a viewing form — it carries dispositions, which no surface does. Calling it a surface borrows a word this corpus has already spent. |
| 2 | Then it is `ui-design`'s by the #174 settlement: assets authored upstream, inlined downstream. | **Refuted** | That settlement scopes `ui-design` to the assets a projector inlines into a self-contained projection. The chat has no assets the corpus can author: no colour, no font, no layout is ours. The harness renders markdown in a terminal, and the Agent cannot style a single byte of it. There is nothing for that craft to hold. |
| 3 | What *is* the chat, then, in this corpus's own terms? | **Refuted as craft content** | Rule-14 property 2: the kernel is "Claude Code (or another CLI inferencing agent), Python at a pinned version, Git as a local repository, and pydantic". The chat is that first row — System Infrastructure, with a declared replacement, observed and pinned in the manifest. Infrastructure is not craft content; Rule-14's Boundaries put the content of The Dyad System outside its own scope, and Rule-11 puts craft content inside a craft root. |
| 4 | Grant `ux-design` instead: the *interaction* is designed — prompt, plan, counter-prompt, disposition. | **Confirmed as observation, refuted as a craft** | The interaction is designed, deliberately and repeatedly: Rule-10's Form (one path, the strongest counter, a reconciliation, one `Y/N` last), Rule-3's counter-prompt forms and batch forms, and four preferences that tune it — `merge-disposition`, `batch-disposition-mode`, `concise-mode`, `delegation`. But every one of those binds **the Agent's process**, and Rule-4's Boundaries settle where such a rule lives: "a rule that binds the Agent's process is an Agent Rule wherever it is written and belongs in the core craft". The craft guard warns on exactly this (`rules.py check_tended`, the Agent-token warning that fires today on three sysadmin rules). A `ux-design` craft would be a second owner of the dyad's own process, which Rule-5 forbids: ownership is never shared. |
| 5 | Steel-man: a craft could own the *presentation* of the interaction while the Rules keep the process — the wording of a counter-prompt, say. | **Refuted** | Rule-10's Form is the wording, and Rule-3 owns the counter-prompt's literal shape (`Y/N: Done with #<id> <title> (merges PR #a, #b)?`). The guards read that shape: `prs.py` parses `d-work #<id>` from a body, `provenance.py` counts dispositions against the row. The wording is load-bearing, not decoration, and splitting it from the process would put a craft's text in a fence's path. |
| 6 | Could the chat become a surface by projecting it — a transcript projection? | **Refuted, and by a different Rule** | Rule-7 property 2 forbids it outright: the raw transcript "is never committed", carries credentials, and is "a property of one kernel and one host, not of the dyad". The one move that would make the chat a projection is the one the corpus already bars. |
| 7 | Then is there anything real in the claim? | **Survives, and it is the finding worth keeping** | Yes: the design of this interaction is **scattered**. It lives in Rule-10's Form, Rule-3's two form clauses, Rule-2's Binding, four preference rows and one falsification record (#113). No single artifact describes the Operator's experience of the dyad, which is why the claim looks unowned when it is merely distributed. The container for that, if wanted, is a play-book or an audit — read by a Rule, never a craft that owns the process. |

**Verdict.** Refuted on both branches, for different reasons. `ui-design` cannot hold the chat
because there is no asset and no projector and the word `surface` means something else here.
`ux-design` cannot hold it because the interaction is the Agent's process, and Rule-4 puts every
rule that binds that process in the core craft. What survives is attack 7: the design exists and is
distributed across two Rules and four preferences, with no one place that reads as its description.

## Revision — 2026-09-25 (the Operator narrows the claim to `ux-design`)
The `ui-design` branch is withdrawn by the Operator and attacks 1, 2 and 6 above stand as the record
of why. The claim under test is now the narrower and much stronger one, and attack 4's two-line
answer is not good enough for it.

**Claim (narrowed):** the current chat surface between Agent and Operator falls under `ux-design`.

| # | Attack | Result | Survivor |
|---|--------|--------|----------|
| 8 | Concede the substance first: is the chat's interaction *designed*? | **Confirmed** | Repeatedly and on purpose. Rule-10 fixes a reply's shape — one path, the strongest counter, a reconciliation, one `Y/N` last. Rule-3 fixes the counter-prompt's literal form and its batch forms. Rule-2's Binding fixes what a `Y` reaches. #113 changed the reply to one synthesis per prompt; #17 added batching; #41 chose `with-done`; #106 reshaped the release question. Every one of those is a user-experience decision, argued and falsified. The claim's observation is right. |
| 9 | So name the craft that owns it — and check whether it is missing. | **Refuted: the craft exists and is the core one** | A craft is "one unit of reusable practice … installed into any dyad system". The reusable practice of *this interaction* is exactly what `dyad-operator` is: the vocabulary calls the core craft's distributable form "frame, Rules, vocabulary, guards, **preference schema**, templates". Rules 2, 3, 9 and 10 are not incidentally about experience; they are interaction design — who may dispose, when a question is asked, how a proposal is framed, what a reply must carry. `ux-design` is not a missing craft. It is a second name for the core craft. |
| 10 | Then the knobs, at least, are a separable concern: how *many* questions, how long a reply, who merges. | **Refuted: that concern is already a layer, and it is Operator-owned** | Those are preferences — `merge-disposition`, `batch-disposition-mode`, `concise-mode`, `delegation`, `ledger-pr-merge` — and the preference layer is deliberately not craft content: `preferences-corpus/` is Operator-owned, the Agent proposes changes by PR and never sets a value. Moving the knobs into a craft would take them from the Operator and hand them to an installable tree. The layering is the design. |
| 11 | Then the rendering: width, colour, keybindings, what the terminal does with markdown. | **Refuted: not ours at any level** | Rule-14 property 4: everything the package invokes that is not kernel is a library row, and **The World is observed, never pinned**. The harness's rendering is The World. No craft can own what no Rule may pin. |
| 12 | Split the difference: a Tended `ux-design` craft that only advises — heuristics, no binding rule. | **Refuted twice over** | #174's attack 4 already settled that a craft which is only prose is not a craft (Rule-11 property 1, Rule-12 property 1). And any rule it did write would trip `rules.py check_tended`, the craft guard's Agent-process warning — the one firing today on three sysadmin rules for the words "the Agent" and "counter-prompt". A craft cannot hold this without becoming the thing Rule-4 sends back to the core. |
| 13 | Test it against history rather than against the Rules: how has a UX change actually been made here? | **Refuted empirically** | Four times in this ledger, and never once through a craft: #41 chose `merge-disposition`; #17 added batch disposition as a preference plus clauses in Rules 2 and 3; #113 added `concise-mode` as a preference plus clauses in Rules 3 and 10; #106 reshaped the release counter-prompt inside Rule-11. The route is always the same pair — a preference for the knob, an Agent Rule for the invariant. A craft has never been the vehicle, and nothing in the log suggests one was wanted. |
| 14 | Is anything left over that a `ux-design` craft *could* own? | **Survives, scoped — and it is not the chat** | One thing: how a *new* Operator learns this system — onboarding, the README, worked examples, the order in which Rules should be read. That is documentation practice, it is reusable, it binds no process, and it would pass the craft guard. It is a real candidate for a craft. It is not the chat surface, and calling it `ux-design` would be the same borrowed word all over again. |

**Verdict (narrowed claim).** Refuted, and more interestingly than the `ui-design` branch. The chat's
interaction *is* designed, and the design is already owned by a three-part partition that nothing is
missing from: the **core craft** holds the invariants (Rules 2, 3, 9, 10), the **preference layer**
holds the knobs and is Operator-owned on purpose, and **The World** holds the rendering and is
observed rather than pinned. `ux-design` names the whole of that partition, which is why it feels
unowned — no single artifact is called by its name. A craft cannot take any of the three parts
without taking it from a named owner.

What survives from the original attack 7 and from attack 14 together: the design is distributed and
undescribed. The container for a description is an audit or a play-book, and a craft-shaped gap
exists only for *teaching* the system, never for running it.

## Revision 2 — 2026-09-25 (the Operator's analogy: `sysux` beside `sysarch`, `syseng`, `sysadmin`)
The Operator asks what is in the core craft, observes that three `sys*` crafts already stand beside
it, and argues that the Operator's experience of the CLI interface is a fourth — `sysux`, or
`sysdialog`. This is the strongest form the claim has taken, because it stops asserting a gap and
starts citing a precedent that is real: all three of those crafts were **extracted from the core**
(#155 sysadmin, #160 sysarch, #162 syseng). The question is whether the chat is extractable by the
same test.

**What is in the core craft today** (`dyad/`, 0.9.1): 19 Agent Rules, the frame `CLAUDE.md`, the
Agent vocabulary (88 terms), 15 guards, 7 scripts, 2 play-books, 2 run-books, the infrastructure
manifest, the preference schema, templates, hooks, `bin/dyad`, and the Rules' own falsification
records. Five Rules are kernels of Rules whose content was extracted (11, 12, 14, 16, 20); three
more split their content out and kept a process kernel (8, 18, 19).

| # | Attack | Result | Survivor |
|---|--------|--------|----------|
| 15 | The precedent is real, so apply the corpus's own extraction test rather than an analogy. | **Confirmed as the right test** | Rule-5's Sets states it: "a kernel is core because **Rule-2's Binding or Rule-3's completion evidence relies on it**". Every extraction so far obeyed it — `sysadmin` took the host's class table and change-log columns, `sysarch` took guard layout and projection, `syseng` took naming, determinism, idempotence and the test mapping; each left behind the clause a `Y` or a completion reply leans on. |
| 16 | Apply it to the chat, honestly, clause by clause. | **Splits — and the split is the answer** | Rule-3's counter-prompt forms and Rule-2's Binding fail the test for extraction: Binding says a `Y` "binds only to the PR(s) or action(s) named in the question it answers", so the question's literal form is what a ratification event resolves against, and `prs.py` and `provenance.py` parse that form. Those stay core under any decomposition. **Rule-10's Form does not obviously fail it** — neither Binding nor the completion evidence cites it — so by Rule-5's test alone, the reply's shape is extractable. The Operator's analogy survives that far, and no further. |
| 17 | Then extract Rule-10's Form into `sysux` and be done. | **Refuted by the other criterion, which the three crafts also obey** | Rule-4's Boundaries: "a rule that binds the Agent's process is an Agent Rule **wherever it is written** and belongs in the core craft" (#52, #153 attack 11), enforced as a warning by `rules.py check_tended`. The three `sys*` crafts pass that test — their rules govern a host, an architecture, an engineering discipline, not the Agent's turn-taking. The guard is warning today on three `sysadmin` rules precisely because they drift toward the Agent's process, and the corpus treats that as a smell to be reported. A `sysux` craft inverts the test: **every** rule it could hold binds the Agent's process, so every rule would trip the guard by construction. It is definitionally the residue Rule-4 sends back. |
| 18 | Read the three crafts as a partition of the whole system, in which case a fourth is owed. | **Refuted** | They are not a partition of the system; they are three extractions of *content* from Rules that kept their process kernels. `sysarch` holds how guards are built and surfaces projected; `syseng` how code is named, made deterministic and tested; `sysadmin` how a host is changed and a server operated. None of them is "the Agent's side of the conversation", and nothing in #153's craft-instantiation criteria treats the set as exhaustive. The core is not what is left over after the crafts; the crafts are what could leave the core without breaking a `Y`. |
| 19 | The name is the tell: `sysux` or `sysdialog` fits the pattern, so the concept must. | **Refuted** | The pattern in those names is a *practice with a Tended discipline* — architecture, engineering, administration — each of which an Operator could tend without the dyad existing. Dialogue between Operator and Agent has no existence outside the dyad; it is the dyad. A name that fits a series is not evidence of a member. |
| 20 | Steel-man the residue: is there a `sysux`-shaped thing that passes both tests? | **Survives, scoped — twice found now** | Yes, and it is not the chat's rules: how a *new* Operator learns this system — onboarding, worked examples, the order the Rules should be read in, what a first d-work looks like. It binds no process, would pass `check_tended`, is reusable across systems, and is genuinely missing. Attack 14 found it from the other direction. If the Operator wants `sysux` to exist, that is what it can hold, and its rules would be Tended. |
| 21 | Cost of being wrong, each way. | **Survives, scoped** | Extracting the chat's rules would put craft text in the path of two fences (`prs.py` reads the citation form, `provenance.py` counts dispositions against the row) and make a craft version bump able to change what a `Y` means — the failure `check_drift` and Rule-11 property 4 exist to prevent elsewhere. Not extracting costs one thing only: no artifact carries the name, which is the complaint that started this d-work. |

**Verdict (the analogy).** The precedent is real and the test it implies is the corpus's own. Applied
honestly, it splits: Rule-3's forms and Rule-2's Binding are core under any decomposition, while
Rule-10's Form passes Rule-5's extraction test — and then fails Rule-4's, which every existing craft
passes and which a `sysux` craft would fail by construction, since every rule it could hold binds the
Agent's process. `sysux` is admissible only as the thing attacks 14 and 20 both found: teaching the
system, not running it.
